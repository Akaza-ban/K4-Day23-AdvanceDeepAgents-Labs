# Video and Multimodal Generation: Foundations, Architectures, Benchmarks and Open Problems

## TL;DR

- Modern video generation is built on a four-step stack that was assembled almost entirely between 2020 and 2022: denoising diffusion [1], diffusion in a compressed autoencoder latent space [2], classifier-free guidance for controllable conditioning [3], and the factorized space–time 3D U-Net that first made video diffusion practical [4].
- The field has since converged on a single dominant recipe — patchified latents, a transformer backbone, and adaLN-style conditioning — instantiated by DiT [5] and extended to spacetime latent patches by Sora [6], Vidu [7], W.A.L.T [8], Movie Gen [9], Open-Sora 2.0 [10] and the Wan family [11]. The main surviving architectural schism is diffusion versus autoregressive next-token generation [12][13][14].
- A parallel "unified model" track now generates text, images, video and audio in one network, either by early-fusion tokenization (Chameleon [15], Emu3 [13]) or by hybrid objectives that pair a next-token loss on text with a diffusion loss on continuous image latents (Transfusion [16]); industrial systems such as GPT-4o image generation and Gemini's Nano Banana family have adopted native, autoregressive image generation [17][18][19].
- Empirical evaluation has moved decisively away from FVD, which is biased toward per-frame quality and barely sensitive to motion [20][21][22], toward multi-dimensional human-aligned suites such as VBench's 16 dimensions [23] and VBench-2.0's 18 intrinsic-faithfulness capabilities [24], and toward MLLM critics such as VideoScore (Spearman 77.1 with human judgement) [25].
- The strongest "world model" claim is not supported by the evidence: the best models achieve only 39.6% joint caption-and-physics adherence on VideoPhy [26], 32.6% on the action-centric VideoPhy-2 [27], and 29.5/100 on Physics-IQ [28], with a systematic study showing out-of-distribution physics error an order of magnitude worse than in-distribution error and no scaling fix [29].

## Background

Video generation is the temporal generalization of image generation, and its foundations are inherited almost wholesale from the image literature. DDPM defines a parameterized Markov chain trained by variational inference to reverse a Gaussian noising process; an epsilon-prediction parameterization connects it to denoising score matching, and the simplified objective attained a CIFAR-10 FID of 3.17 and IS of 9.46 at the time [1]. Latent Diffusion Models then moved diffusion out of pixel space into the latent space of a pretrained autoencoder with perceptual and semantic compression, adding cross-attention so that a single backbone could be conditioned on text or boxes, reaching a CelebA-HQ 256² FID of 5.11 while cutting training and inference cost relative to pixel-space diffusion [2]. Classifier-free guidance supplies the third foundation: jointly training conditional and unconditional score estimators in one network (randomly dropping the condition with probability p_uncond) and sampling with ϵ̃ = (1+w)·ϵ(z,c) − w·ϵ(z) yields best FID at small guidance weights (0.1–0.3) and best IS at w ≥ 4, exposing a fidelity/diversity trade-off without a separate classifier [3]. This mechanism became the default control knob for essentially every subsequent video model [30][31].

The pivotal step from images to video is the Video Diffusion Model, which converts each 2D 3×3 convolution into a "space-only" 1×3×3 3D convolution and inserts temporal attention blocks after each spatial attention block using relative position embeddings — a factorized space–time 3D U-Net. Because the factorization is explicit, the temporal attention can simply be masked, letting one model train jointly on images and video, which improved sample quality; classifier-free guidance plus a new reconstruction-guided conditional sampling enabled long-video and temporal-super-resolution extension [4]. Two competing design philosophies then emerged. The diffusion branch scaled cascades: Make-A-Video built T2V from a frozen T2I model with pseudo-3D convolutions and temporal attention over unpaired video, requiring no paired text–video data [30], while Imagen Video used a cascade of seven video diffusion sub-models (one base, three spatial super-resolution, three temporal super-resolution) totalling roughly 11.6B diffusion parameters over a frozen T5 encoder to produce 128 frames at 1280×768 and 24 fps [31]. The autoregressive branch instead tokenized video: VideoGPT combined a VQ-VAE with axial attention and a GPT-like prior to reach BAIR FVD 103 [32]; NÜWA built a 3D transformer encoder–decoder with 3D Nearby Attention and showed that multi-task pretraining improved MSR-VTT CLIPSIM from 0.2314 to 0.2379 and FVD from 52.98 to 51.81 [33]; Phenaki introduced the causal-in-time C-ViViT tokenizer with a MaskGIT-style masked transformer to generate minutes-long, variable-length, story-prompted videos [34]; MAGVIT reduced UCF-101 class-conditional FVD from 332 to 76 by combining a 3D VQ tokenizer with multi-task masked token modeling [35]; and MAGVIT-v2 showed that with lookup-free quantization and a shared image/video vocabulary, an LLM can beat diffusion on ImageNet 512² (FID 1.91 versus 2.65, a 28% reduction) with fewer decoding steps and compression quality on par with VVC [36].

Two efficiency innovations completed the toolbox. Cascaded spatial and temporal super-resolution was the earliest scaling strategy [30][31], and it was later replaced by distillation: consistency models map any point on the probability-flow ODE trajectory directly to its origin, enabling one-step generation by construction with FID 3.55 on CIFAR-10 and 6.20 on ImageNet 64², beating progressive distillation [37], while latent consistency models distilled classifier-free-guided Stable Diffusion through an augmented PF-ODE with a skipping-step schedule, needing only about 32 A100 GPU-hours to reach a 768² two-to-four-step model [38]. In parallel, flow matching replaced score matching with a simulation-free conditional-flow objective regressing a target vector field along optimal-transport straight-line paths [39], and the rectified-flow transformer (SD3) combined logit-normal timestep sampling with an MM-DiT backbone of separate text and image streams at 8B parameters and 5×10²² FLOPs, showing rectified flow to be more sample-efficient at low step counts [40]. Wan explicitly builds on this DiT-plus-flow-matching recipe with a 3D causal spatio-temporal VAE, in 1.3B and 14B sizes, the smaller running in 8.19GB of VRAM [11].

## Theme 1: From factorized U-Nets to spacetime diffusion transformers — the architectural convergence

The single most consequential architectural result in this literature is that a plain transformer over latent patches outperforms the U-Net that diffusion inherited from image synthesis. DiT replaced the U-Net with a Vision-Transformer-style backbone operating on latent patches, with four configurations (DiT-S, DiT-B, DiT-L, DiT-XL) spanning roughly 33M to 675M parameters and about 0.4 to 119 GFLOPs per forward pass, and identified adaLN-Zero as the winning conditioning mechanism: an MLP regresses dimension-wise scale and shift plus a residual scaling initialized to zero so that each block begins as the identity. Comparing four DiT-XL/2 block designs of similar scale — in-context conditioning at 119.4 GFLOPs, cross-attention at 137.6 GFLOPs, and adaLN and adaLN-Zero both at 118.6 GFLOPs — adaLN-Zero was the best, reaching FID-50K 2.27 on class-conditional ImageNet 256×256, beating the prior LDM result [5]. Critically, quality tracked GFLOPs rather than raw parameter count — shrinking the patch size from 8 to 2 leaves parameter count roughly constant while raising GFLOPs and lowering FID, and configs with similar GFLOPs obtain similar FID (DiT-S/2 ≈ DiT-B/4) [5].

Video models then generalized this to three dimensions. Latte encoded each frame with a pretrained VAE and studied four space–time factorization variants, with the interleaved-fusion variant reaching FID 5.02 on FaceForensics and IS 68.53 on UCF101 (73.31/3.87 with image–video joint training) at 256×256 [41]. Snap Video argued explicitly that U-Nets scale poorly for video and replaced them with FIT-style transformer blocks that treat space–time as one compressed 1D latent: a 500M-parameter FIT trained 3.31× faster and inferred 4.49× faster than a 284M U-Net while scaling to 3.9B parameters [42]. Vidu adopted a U-ViT backbone treating time, text condition and noisy 3D patches all as tokens with long skip connections, generating 1080p video up to 16 seconds in a single pass [7]. W.A.L.T used a causal 3D CNN encoder–decoder to jointly compress images and videos into a unified latent space and alternated non-overlapping spatial and spatiotemporal window attention, which is up to 2× faster than full attention, with a three-model cascade producing 512×896 at 8 fps without classifier-free guidance [8]. Sora's diffusion transformer operates directly on spacetime latent patches with images treated as single-frame videos, trained jointly on variable resolution, duration and aspect ratio, supporting 1920×1080 and 1080×1920 up to about one minute and demonstrating emergent 3D consistency and multi-shot generation of a consistent character [6]. At the largest scale reported, Movie Gen Video is a 30B-parameter LLaMA3-style transformer trained with flow matching over a temporal autoencoder with 8×8×8 spatial–temporal compression, a 73K-token context for 16 seconds at 16 fps and 768px, pretrained jointly on O(100)M videos and O(1)B images [9]. Open-Sora 2.0 shows how far this recipe can be compressed in cost: an 11B-parameter model (19 double-stream plus 38 single-stream layers, dim 3072, 24 heads, patch size 2) initialized from the 11B Flux text-to-image model, trained for roughly $200k (about 4,160 GPU-days on H200) and reaching VBench and human-preference levels comparable to HunyuanVideo and Runway Gen-3 Alpha [10].

The autoregressive line has kept pace on efficiency and scaling. VAR reframes autoregressive image generation as coarse-to-fine next-scale prediction rather than raster-scan next-token prediction, starting from a 1×1 token map and predicting progressively higher-resolution maps with block-wise causal attention and KV-caching, improving the AR baseline on ImageNet 256×256 from FID 18.65 to 1.73 and IS 80.4 to 350.2 with 20× faster inference, and reporting power-law scaling with Pearson correlation near −0.998; it needs 350 training epochs versus DiT-XL/2's 1400, and DiT shows marginal or negative gains beyond 675M parameters while VAR keeps improving [14]. Infinity scaled bitwise autoregressive modeling with an infinite-vocabulary classifier (predicting d bits instead of 2^d classes, 0.13M parameters versus 8.8T for a conventional 2^32-class classifier at hidden dimension 2048) and a bitwise multi-scale tokenizer with vocabulary up to 2^64, surpassing SD3-Medium (GenEval 0.62→0.73, ImageReward 0.87→0.96, 66% win rate) and generating 1024×1024 in 0.8 s [43]. Emu3 shows the same principle applied across modalities: a single transformer trained from scratch purely with next-token prediction over tokenized image, text and video beats SDXL on text-to-image and is competitive with LLaVA-1.6 on vision-language understanding, using a MoVQGAN-based tokenizer that compresses a 4×512×512 clip into 4096 discrete tokens from a 32,768-entry codebook [13].

The concrete comparison across these families is instructive. On the diffusion side, generation cost is dominated by sampling steps, which distillation attacks directly: adversarial diffusion distillation compresses SD/SDXL to one to four steps by combining adversarial and score-distillation losses, beating LCM-XL at one step and matching SDXL at four [44]; latent adversarial diffusion distillation moves the discriminator into latent space using the teacher's own generative features, letting SD3-Turbo match the 8B SD3 in four unguided steps at megapixel multi-aspect resolution [45]; and for video, DOLLAR combines variational score distillation, consistency distillation and latent reward-model fine-tuning to produce a four-step student scoring 82.57 on VBench for 10-second (128-frame, 12 fps) video, up to 278.6× faster than the 50-step teacher [46]. On the autoregressive side the cost is token count, which the tokenizer attacks: lookup-free quantization removes the codebook embedding dimension entirely to permit large vocabularies that improve LM generation quality and enable a single shared vocabulary for images and videos [36], while finite scalar quantization projects the representation to fewer than ten dimensions and rounds each channel to a small fixed set of values, giving an implicit codebook with roughly 100% usage, no commitment loss, no codebook collapse, and only a 0.5–3% metric drop versus VQ as a drop-in replacement [47]. Transfusion deliberately refuses this trade-off for images, arguing that continuous VAE latent patches (with no quantization) avoid discretization information loss and that this scales significantly better than quantizing images into discrete tokens [16]. Sequence-level engineering complements both: Open-Sora 2.0 uses context parallelism (CP=4 at 768px) and selective activation checkpointing to cut 768×768 inference from 1656 s on one GPU to 276 s on eight GPUs at 50 steps [10], and Movie Gen stages context length and parallelization to reach 73K tokens [9].

## Theme 2: Unified and multimodal generation — one model, many modalities

The second major research thrust is to stop treating video, image, text and audio as separate modelling problems. Three strategies dominate. The first is fully token-based early fusion: Chameleon quantizes images into discrete tokens and applies the same transformer to image and text token sequences with no modality-specific encoders or decoders, introducing query–key normalization and revised layer-norm placement for stable mixed-modal training, and matching or beating Gemini Pro and GPT-4V on a long-form mixed-modal human evaluation while matching Mixtral 8x7B and Gemini-Pro on text benchmarks [15]. Emu3 is the purest instance of this philosophy, training a single transformer from scratch with tokenizer-only next-token prediction and no diffusion or CLIP-plus-LLM composition [13]. Show-o unifies multimodal understanding and generation in one transformer by combining autoregressive modeling with discrete diffusion, supporting VQA, text-to-image, text-guided inpainting and extrapolation, and mixed-modality generation at performance comparable to task-specific models of equal or larger size [48]. AnyGPT similarly uses discrete representations in a multimodal LLM to process and generate across modalities with performance on par with specialized models [49], and 4M-21 co-trains a single any-to-any vision model over many modalities and tasks via discrete tokenization [50].

The second strategy is hybrid objectives. Transfusion trains one transformer over mixed-modality sequences by combining the language-modeling loss (next-token prediction) for text with a diffusion (DDPM) loss for images, so that text tokens receive causal attention while image patches receive bidirectional intra-image attention; it was pretrained up to 7B parameters and 2T multimodal tokens on a 50/50 text/image mixture, and with modality-specific encode/decode layers an image can be compressed to just 16 patches [16]. The third is decoupled encoding. Janus and Janus-Pro route understanding through SigLIP-L and generation through a VQ tokenizer with downsample rate 16 into a single autoregressive transformer on DeepSeek-LLM 1.5B/7B bases; Janus-Pro-7B reports 79.2 on MMBench, GenEval 0.80 (versus 0.61 for Janus, 0.67 for DALL·E 3 and 0.74 for SD3-Medium) and 84.19 on DPG-Bench [51][52]. Industry has largely converged on native generation: OpenAI states that 4o image generation is an autoregressive model, explicitly contrasted with the diffusion approach of DALL·E, embedded natively deep in the omnimodal GPT-4o architecture with reliable in-image text rendering [17][18], while Gemini's Nano Banana line (Gemini 2.5 Flash Image, Gemini 3 Pro Image, Gemini 3.1 Flash Image) supports conversational editing, up to 14 reference images, 1K to 4K output, Search grounding and SynthID watermarking, and notably produces interleaved text-and-image output inside a single response [19][53].

Joint audio–video generation has been approached in two opposite ways. VATT established the single-backbone extreme: a convolution-free transformer taking raw video, audio and text, trained end-to-end from scratch with multimodal contrastive losses and even a modality-agnostic shared-weight variant, setting records without supervised pretraining including 82.1% top-1 on Kinetics-400 and 39.4% mAP on AudioSet [54]. Meta Movie Gen takes the opposite "cast of models" approach: Movie Gen Video is the 30B flow-matching transformer, while Movie Gen Audio is a separate 13B model generating 48kHz sound effects and music synchronized to video, pretrained on O(1)M hours of audio, with post-training yielding Personalized Movie Gen Video and instruction-based Movie Gen Edit [9]. NVIDIA's Cosmos platform bundles diffusion-based and autoregressive world foundation models with a visual Cosmos Tokenizer claimed at 8× total compression and 12× faster processing than prior leading tokenizers, plus Cosmos-Reason1 multimodal LLMs at 7B, 8B and 56B with physically grounded responses [55][56][57].

Instruction-based and reference-guided editing is where unification is most practically tested. Recent work includes multimodal instruction-based editing combined with subject-driven generation via feature mixing and index encoding [58], unified architectures for instruction- and reference-guided video editing, region-constrained in-context generation for instructional video editing, and VLM-guided encoding with reward optimization; the trend is toward self-rewarding unified models, where a model's own understanding module supplies the reward signal for its generation module to close the understanding-to-generation transfer gap [59]. Tokenizer choice remains the decisive design variable across all of these systems: the discrete route requires high-usage codebooks such as LFQ [36] and FSQ [47], whereas the continuous route uses VAE latents and accepts a different train/inference trade-off [16].

## Theme 3: Evaluation, data and the physics gap — measuring what video and multimodal models actually do

Evaluation of video generation has undergone a methodological crisis and a partial recovery. The field's founding metric, Fréchet Video Distance, was introduced with a claim of 74.9–81.0% agreement with human judgement [21], but subsequent work showed it is biased toward per-frame quality and weakly sensitive to motion: adding severe temporal corruption can yield a better (lower) FVD of 310.52 versus 317.10, FVD can be roughly halved by sampling motion-free videos from the perceptual null space, and the bias is attributable to supervised I3D features, with self-supervised VideoMAE-v2 features reducing it [20]. A structural critique identifies three failures of FVD — non-Gaussianity of the I3D feature space, insensitivity of I3D to temporal distortion, and impractically large sample requirements — and proposes JEDi using V-JEPA features with MMD, which needs only 16% of the samples while increasing human alignment by 34% on average [22]. Practitioner benchmarks reached the same conclusion from the human side: EvalCrafter built 700 diverse T2V prompts and found raw objective metrics correlate poorly with user opinion, with CLIP-Score Spearman only 6.3, IS 9.9, and negative correlations for celebrity-ID, OCR and colour scores, which motivated regressing a blended score against subjective ratings from 7 annotators and 8,647 feedback scores [60]. FETV's 619-prompt, multi-aspect and temporal-aware design collected 28,116 ratings and similarly concluded that "existing automatic metrics (e.g., CLIPScore and FVD) correlate poorly with human evaluation" [61].

The replacement paradigm is multi-dimensional, human-anchored benchmarking. VBench decomposes video generation quality into 16 disentangled dimensions organized under video quality (temporal and frame-wise) and video-condition consistency (semantics and style), with about 100 prompts per dimension plus an 800-prompt suite across eight content categories, shipped with human-preference annotations [23]. VBench-2.0 extends this to "intrinsic faithfulness" with 5 broad categories (human fidelity, controllability, creativity, physics, commonsense) and 18 fine-grained capability dimensions, combining VLM/LLM generalists with specialist anomaly detectors and extensive human preference annotation [24]. Compositional evaluation is a recurring stress test: T2V-CompBench uses 7 categories × 200 prompts with MLLM-based, detection-based and tracking-based metrics and concludes that compositional T2V is "highly challenging for current models" [62], while GenAI-Bench's 1,600 compositional prompts and 38,400+ human ratings expose a basic-versus-advanced reasoning gap, with DALL·E 3 scoring about 4.3/5 on basic compositional prompts but dropping to about 3.4/5 on advanced ones involving counting, comparison, logic and differentiation [63]. The newest layer is MLLM-as-judge: VideoScore released VideoFeedback with human multi-aspect scores over 37.6K videos from 11 generative models and reached Spearman 77.1 on VideoFeedback-test, beating the prior best by about 50 points, with 78.5 pairwise accuracy on GenAI-Bench video preference, whereas naive GPT-4o or Gemini-1.5 prompting achieved only about 17–23 average correlation [25].

Data is the hidden variable behind all of these numbers. WebVid-10M contains roughly 10.7M web alt-text video–caption pairs across about 52K video hours [64], HD-VILA-100M provides 100M clips from 3.3M source videos at 720p with ASR-annotated captions, and Panda-70M distils 70.8M semantically coherent clips (166.8K hours, about 13.2 words per caption) from 3.8M HD-VILA videos using multiple cross-modality teacher models with a retrieval-based caption selector [64]. The captioning finding is significant: a human study for Panda-70M found no single captioning model produced a good caption for more than 31% of videos, whereas jointly 84.7% could receive at least one good caption, which is a direct argument for multi-teacher pipelines [64]. InternVid scales further with over 7M YouTube videos yielding 234M clips and about 4.1B words of LLM-generated multi-scale captions [65]. Recaptioning has become a standard training technique: OpenAI trained a bespoke CLIP-conditioned captioner to produce descriptive synthetic captions and trained DALL·E 3 on a 95% synthetic to 5% ground-truth caption mixture to improve prompt following [66]. The legal and ethical exposure of this data regime is now concrete rather than hypothetical: a Stanford Internet Observatory investigation identified 3,226 dataset entries of suspected CSAM within LAION-5B using PhotoDNA and nearest-neighbour verification, reported them to NCMEC and C3P, and LAION temporarily took down its datasets citing GDPR Article 17 [67][68]. In *Getty Images v Stability AI*, Getty abandoned its training and output copyright claims mid-trial and the UK court largely rejected the copyright claim in November 2025, while Stability admitted that "at least some" Getty images were used in training [69].

The most consequential empirical finding for the field's self-image concerns physics. OpenAI's own Sora report concedes the model "does not accurately model the physics of many basic interactions, like glass shattering" while simultaneously claiming that scale produces "simulators of the physical world" [6], and Meta reports that Movie Gen's pretrained video model can reason about object motion, subject–object interactions, geometry, camera motion and physics [9]. Benchmarks sharply qualify these claims. VideoPhy, built on 688 human-verified captions, found the best model at the time (CogVideoX-5B) produced videos that both followed the caption and obeyed physics in only 39.6% of instances, with several models scoring below 20% [26]. VideoPhy-2, an action-centric benchmark built from 197 seed actions expanded into 3,940 detailed prompts, found the best model (Wan2.1-14B) at only 32.6% joint performance, dropping to 22% on a hard subset, with models struggling most on conservation laws of mass and momentum [27]. Physics-IQ evaluated 396 videos across 66 scenarios spanning solid mechanics, fluid dynamics, optics, thermodynamics and magnetism against Sora, Runway Gen-3, Pika, Lumiere, SVD and VideoPoet: the best model (VideoPoet multiframe) scored only 29.5 against a 100-point physical-variance baseline, and physical understanding was unrelated to visual realism [28]. A systematic study trained diffusion video models on a controlled 2D physics testbed (30K to 3M examples, 22M to 310M parameters) and found perfect in-distribution generalization but out-of-distribution velocity error of about 0.427 versus 0.012 in-distribution — an order of magnitude worse — with scaling giving little or no improvement, and a "case-based" rather than rule-based generalization ordering (colour ≫ size ≫ velocity ≫ shape) [29]. Together these results indicate that high visual fidelity and physical understanding are largely decoupled capabilities, and that the world-simulator framing is currently an aspiration rather than a measurement.

## Trends and open problems

**Convergence with an unresolved schism.** The DiT/diffusion-transformer recipe — patchified latents, adaLN-style conditioning, transformer backbone — is now shared by Sora [6], Vidu [7], W.A.L.T [8], Movie Gen [9], Open-Sora 2.0 [10], Wan [11] and Cosmos [57], which is the clearest sign of field-level convergence. The live disagreement is diffusion versus autoregressive next-token generation, where VAR and Infinity report cleaner scaling laws and 20–45× inference advantages over DiT on image tasks [14][43] while Transfusion reports that continuous diffusion losses scale better than discrete quantization [16]. That disagreement is now being resolved empirically rather than rhetorically, and the resolution matters because it determines whether future video models inherit LLM infrastructure (KV-caching, sequence parallelism, tokenizer research) or diffusion infrastructure (distillation, guidance, schedulers). A related open question is the absence of a widely accepted scaling law for video: VAR reports power-law behaviour with Pearson near −0.998 [14], DiT showed strong GFLOPs-to-FID correlation but weak parameter scaling above about 675M [5], and Cosmos explores diffusion and autoregressive pretraining as parallel scalable approaches without a unified law [57].

**Physical plausibility and causality.** As Theme 3 documents, the gap between perceptual realism and physical correctness is large and, on current evidence, not closing with scale alone: an order-of-magnitude in-distribution to out-of-distribution error gap that persists as models grow from 22M to 310M parameters [29], joint physics-and-caption adherence below 40% on the best contemporaneous models [26] and below 33% on action-centric tasks [27], and a 29.5/100 score on Physics-IQ with no correlation to visual realism [28]. The open question is whether architectural changes — explicit 3D or physics-informed representations, causal rather than correlational objectives, or reinforcement learning from physical-consistency rewards — can deliver what scale has not.

**Long video, identity drift and controllability.** Current systems achieve duration through autoregressive extension — VDM's reconstruction-guided sampling [4], Phenaki's frozen-past-token generation for arbitrary-length story videos [34], VideoPoet's 10-second extension conditioned on the last second [12], and Sora's forward and backward extension [6] — and identity preservation across shots is claimed as an emergent capability [6]. But consistency degrades over time, and interactive world models make the constraint explicit: Genie 3 sustains several minutes of interactive, real-time, 720p generation at 20–24 fps with visual memory reaching back about one minute, with consistency emerging without any explicit 3D representation [70], while the Project Genie prototype imposes a 60-second generation limit and acknowledges gaps in prompt adherence and physics control [70]. World Labs' Marble takes the opposite route, producing explicit, persistent, exportable 3D worlds (Gaussian splats and meshes) with free-viewpoint navigation rather than frame-by-frame video [71]. Which representation wins — implicit frame-by-frame generation or explicit 3D — is unresolved and determines downstream usability for robotics and simulation. Downstream use is already happening: Genie Envisioner applies video diffusion foundation models as world simulators for robotic manipulation [72], and Cosmos is explicitly marketed as a physical-AI platform spanning VLM, policy backbone and physics-grounded simulator roles [55][56], raising the stakes on the physics evidence above.

**Compute, cost and accessibility.** The cost curve has moved dramatically in two years, from billion-dollar-scale proprietary training runs with undisclosed compute [6][73] to Open-Sora 2.0's demonstrably commercial-level 11B model at roughly $200k [10], and from 50-step inference to four-step distilled students with 278.6× speedups [46]. Yet reporting remains uneven: OpenAI disclosed no model or implementation details for Sora [6][73], and a cross-topic study of reproducibility in generative and LLM research flags model availability, software decay, data governance, hardware cost, and "evaluation drift" (benchmarks run against drifting model versions, APIs and judges) as systemic problems, with foundation-model papers scoring worst on hardware approachability at 0.77 [74]. A large-scale analysis of human-evaluation protocols spanning 284 manually reviewed papers and over 1,800 LLM-assisted ones found widespread under-reporting that creates ambiguity about what was measured and by whom [74], which compounds the metric problems documented in Theme 3 [20][22][60][61].

**Provenance, safety and regulation.** Technical and legal infrastructure for synthetic-media disclosure has arrived in the last two years. Google's SynthID embeds imperceptible watermarks across image, audio, text and video, has watermarked more than 10 billion pieces of content, survives cropping, filters, frame-rate changes and lossy compression, and launched a SynthID Detector portal with detection integrated into Gemini, Search and Chrome [75]. C2PA Content Credentials provide a cryptographic provenance standard in which assertions roll up into a signed claim and manifest, combining hard cryptographic bindings with soft watermark and derived bindings in a tamper-evident structure using claim signatures and trusted timestamps [76]. Deployment practice is following: OpenAI's Sora 2 system card documents C2PA metadata on all assets plus visible moving watermarks, output-blocking CSAM classifiers and likeness controls [77], and Google's Veo documentation reports state-of-the-art human-perceived quality on the 1,003-prompt MovieGenBench but also documents a bias skew toward lighter skin tones when race is unspecified [78]. Regulation is now binding rather than advisory: EU AI Act Article 50 applies from 2 August 2026 (with marking obligations for pre-existing systems from 2 December 2026), requiring providers of generative systems to mark outputs in a machine-readable, detectable format and deployers of deepfakes to disclose them clearly, supported by a voluntary Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-generated Content [79]; in the United States, 31 states have enacted deepfake-in-politics laws, several of which have been challenged in court, and some states such as Colorado and Utah require provenance or metadata disclosure [80]. The open problems here are genuine and unsolved: watermark robustness under adversarial removal and re-encoding, the absence of a universal cross-vendor detection standard, the fact that bias findings such as the Veo skin-tone skew [78] have no accepted mitigation, and the difficulty of provenance for content that is itself a transformation of authentic media.
